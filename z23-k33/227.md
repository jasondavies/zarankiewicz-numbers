# Excluding 227 ones on the 23-square

An admissible 23 × 23 matrix has at most **226 ones**. We use the 218 upper bound on 22 × 23 and the restrictions on 209-one matrices on 21 × 23. Throughout, admissible means that every triple of rows has at most two common ones. The same condition holds for triples of columns. [Rectangular bound](../z22x23-k33/README.md) · [209-one degree bounds](../z21x23-k33/six-columns.md) · [Four-column restriction](../z21x23-k33/four-deficit-seven.md)

## Deleting two nine-rows

Suppose the square has weight 227. All rows and columns have degree at least nine by single-line deletion. Write $a$ and $b$ for the numbers of nine-rows and nine-columns. There are at least three of each since $230-227=3$. Erasing any two nine-rows gives a 209-one matrix with row deficit $D=a-2$. Hence $6\le a\le9$, and transposition gives the same range for $b$. The residual matrix has $q=4$ or $5$ columns of degree eight, with $4\le D\le6$ at $q=4$ and $5\le D\le7$ at $q=5$. Its unchanged row degrees are at most thirteen. If $u,v$ count the twelve- and thirteen-rows, then $2(D+u+3v)\le3q+3$, and a thirteen-row requires $q=5$. The full square has $a-3-2u-3v$ eleven-rows. This gives a short list of profiles on each side.

A nine-column cannot contain two nine-rows, since their deletion would leave degree seven. The transpose shows that the nine-rows and nine-columns induce a matching. Write $m$ for its size and $h_i\in\{0,1\}$ for whether nine-row $i$ is matched. For two nine-rows $i,j$, exactly $q=h_i+h_j+\lambda_{ij}$ columns become degree eight, where $\lambda_{ij}$ counts common ten-columns. Each remaining nine-row meets at most one of these $q$ columns. Consequently three nine-rows have at most one common ten-column. [Restrictions on short rows](../z21x23-k33/README.md)

## Bounds on column supports among the nine-rows

Let $t$ be the number of nine-rows on a column of degree $c$. For $c=10$, $t\ge3$. Otherwise delete two nine-rows missing the column. The surviving ten-column must carry at least three row-deficit units, each supplied by a nine-row. For $c\ge11$, $t\ge c-6$. To see this, delete two nine-rows avoiding the column as far as possible. If $s$ incident rows are deleted and $c-s\ge11$, the residual deficit bound is $t-s\ge c-s-6$. If $c-s\le10$, then for $c=11$ at most one nine-row missed the column, giving $t\ge a-1\ge5$. For $c=12$ both deletions hit the column and $t=a\ge6$. These cover all remaining cases. [Column deficit bounds](../z21x23-k33/eight-columns.md)

Write $T$ for the multiset of support sizes of the ten-columns on the $a$ nine-rows, and $L$ for those of the longer columns. Their sum is $9a-m$. The triple counts from $T$ alone total at most $\binom a3$, and from $T,L$ together at most $2\binom a3$. Summing the identity for $q$ over row pairs gives
$$
\binom a2\min(q)\ \le\ (a-1)m+\sum_{t\in T}\binom t2\ \le\ \binom a2\max(q),
$$
where the permitted values of $q$ come from the row profile. Enforce these necessary integer constraints in both orientations with the same $m$. Only $a=b=7$ and $a=b=8$ remain. Forgetting the order and individual degrees of the longer columns leaves **68** distinct cases $(a,b,m,\{q\},T,L)$.

If two columns of degrees at least ten share $k$ nine-rows, their $2k$ entries, at most $k$ on all the nine-columns by the matching, and at most two per remaining column must supply $9k$ entries. Hence $6k\le42-2b$. This bounds the pairwise intersections of the supports.

## Exhaustion

For each case, place supports of the prescribed longer-column sizes on the $a$ labelled points. At each step points with identical membership in all previous supports can be permuted, so it suffices to choose an initial segment within each such group. Reject a third occurrence of a triple. Then place ten-column supports of the prescribed sizes in fixed order within each size. A triple can occur on at most one ten-column and at most twice overall. Finally, the points have loads eight if matched and nine otherwise. Check the required number of matched points and the exact permitted $q$ on every pair. No case survives. The finite checks use integer operations only.
